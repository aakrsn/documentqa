# The Science of Why We Dream 🧠

Have you ever woken up from a dream and wondered, "Why on earth did my brain create that?"

Maybe you dreamed about flying, having a conversation with someone you haven't met in years, or finding yourself in a completely unfamiliar place that somehow felt real.

What's fascinating is that your brain can construct these elaborate experiences while your body is lying almost motionless in bed.

Let's explore what's happening.

## 1. What actually happens when we dream?

Sleep isn't simply a state where your brain switches off. Your brain remains surprisingly active.

Sleep consists of several stages, broadly divided into:

- NREM sleep: Your body gradually relaxes, and your brain moves through lighter and deeper stages of sleep.
- REM sleep (Rapid Eye Movement): Your brain becomes highly active, your eyes move rapidly, and vivid dreams are particularly common.

During REM sleep:

- Brain regions involved in emotions and memory are active.
- Areas involved in logical reasoning and critical thinking can be less active.
- Your body experiences temporary muscle paralysis, which prevents you from physically acting out most dreams.

This might explain why you can accept completely ridiculous situations in dreams without questioning them.

Imagine dreaming that you're attending a college lecture, but your professor is a talking elephant. Somehow, you just accept it!

## 2. Why do we dream about people from our past?

Have you ever dreamed about an old school friend you haven't spoken to in 10 years?

One explanation is that dreaming involves the reactivation and recombination of memories.

Your brain processes experiences, emotions, and memories during sleep. Elements from different memories can appear together in a dream.

For example:

- You meet an old friend.
- You're suddenly back in your childhood home.
- You're having a conversation that never actually happened.

Your brain may be combining fragments of different experiences rather than replaying a single memory.

However, scientists still don't fully understand why particular people or memories appear in specific dreams.

## 3. Why do we forget dreams so quickly?

This is particularly interesting.

You might wake up remembering an incredibly detailed dream. Five minutes later, you can barely remember what happened.

There are several possible explanations:

- During REM sleep, brain systems involved in forming and storing memories operate differently.
- Dream experiences aren't always encoded into long-term memory effectively.
- Once you wake up, new information and distractions can interfere with recalling the dream.

That's why keeping a dream journal beside your bed can help. Writing down even a few details immediately after waking can improve dream recall.

Yes! It's called Lucid Dreaming.

A lucid dream occurs when you become aware that you're dreaming while the dream is still happening.

Some people report being able to:

- Fly over cities.
- Change their surroundings.
- Choose what happens next.
- Interact consciously with people in their dreams.

Interestingly, lucid dreaming has been studied scientifically, and researchers have even communicated with some lucid dreamers using prearranged eye movements during REM sleep.

## 5. The biggest unanswered question: Why do we dream?

There are several scientific theories:

| Theory                | Explanation                                                                                        |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Memory consolidation  | Dreams may be associated with processing and organizing memories.                                  |
| Emotional processing  | Dreaming may help process emotional experiences.                                                   |
| Threat simulation     | Dreams may provide simulations of threatening situations.                                          |
| Random brain activity | Dreams may emerge partly from spontaneous brain activity that the mind organizes into experiences. |

None of these theories completely explains dreaming. It's an active area of neuroscience research.
